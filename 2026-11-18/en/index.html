Ukraine cuts the road on the Crimean Bridge, two days before the Shenzhen summit between Xi, Trump and Putin

Attack confirmed by Kyiv and Moscow · damage described by Kyiv; Russia admits «damage» and has cut road traffic · 17/11/2026

The bridge linking Russia to the Ukrainian peninsula of Crimea, occupied by Moscow, has lost its road link. Ukraine attacked it with drones and missiles overnight from Monday to Tuesday 17 November, and the Ukrainian General Staff says an arch and a span of the road have collapsed. The railway section is still standing, with one arch damaged. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky called it an «unprecedented» operation. The attack comes two days before Vladimir Putin sits down with Xi Jinping and Donald Trump in Shenzhen, China.

On the Russian side, the authorities admit the attack but give no details of the damage. Russia's Investigative Committee spoke of «damage» and opened a terrorism case. The Moscow-appointed governor of Crimea, Sergei Aksyonov, announced that car traffic had been suspended and that trains were running again on Tuesday afternoon. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov insisted that the bridge «will be rebuilt and will be better than it was» and did not rule out a link between the attack and the three-way summit.

According to Zelensky, it was the final phase of a seven-stage campaign: first, air defences, radars and electronic warfare equipment were destroyed; then long-range drones hit the road, Neptune missiles hit the railway and Flamingo cruise missiles delivered the final blow. The Ukrainian company that makes the Flamingo, Fire Point, claims that around 275 metres of road were destroyed. The AP news agency was unable to verify the videos showing part of the deck in the water. The bridge is seen as a symbol of the illegal annexation of Crimea in 2014 and is used to supply Russian troops.

On the same night, the war continued in both directions. Russia said it had faced 1,441 Ukrainian drones, 800 of them aimed at Moscow, according to the capital's mayor. Ukraine says Russia launched 306 drones and several missiles: at least two people died in Kyiv, where ballistic missiles reportedly targeted bridges over the Dnipro river, and there were more deaths in Chernihiv, Zaporizhzhia and Vinnytsia. In Crimea, Aksyonov said two people were killed in a drone attack in Kerch.

Equinor, the Norwegian oil and gas giant, is being hit by a cyberattack; the company says production has not been affected

Attack confirmed by Equinor (17 Nov) and ongoing · perpetrators unknown · company says there are no signs of data theft · 17/11/2026

Equinor, Norway's largest oil and gas producer, is dealing with a cyberattack. The company announced it on Tuesday 17 November, at 17:55 Lisbon time: the attack, still ongoing, targets «some» of its IT systems. Equinor says production and operations have not been affected, that there are no signs that data has been stolen and that it has already informed the authorities.

Continue readingShow less

What is known beyond the statement comes mainly from the Reuters news agency. An employee told it that the company's internal email had stopped working, and the Norwegian news agency NTB reported problems with internal apps and systems. Equinor has not confirmed these details and a spokesperson declined to give more information. Norway's National Security Authority (NSM, the body responsible for protecting critical infrastructure) said it was aware of the incident and was providing support where needed.

Why does this matter? Equinor is controlled by the Norwegian state and is described as Europe's largest supplier of natural gas, so any failure in its systems worries the countries that depend on that gas. So far there are no signs of supply cuts. On 27 October, the NSM had raised the country's digital risk level to «high», the second highest on a five-point scale, and warned that successful attacks against Norwegian organisations had tripled in six months.

What we still don't know: who is behind the attack, how the attackers got in, which systems were hit and when the situation will be resolved. The company says it will give more information when it has it. The conclusion that no data was stolen is, for now, a preliminary assessment, not the result of a completed investigation.

NSA's secret network goes down in a week of outages in the US; Government insists there was no cyberattack

Outage confirmed by the NSA («routine maintenance») · Government and senator say there are no signs of an attack · investigation continues · 17/11/2026

The internal, classified network of the US National Security Agency (NSA), used by staff to exchange secret information, was down on Tuesday 17 November. The outage was first reported by CNN and confirmed to the AP by a defence official. The NSA put it down to «routine maintenance» and said mission-essential capabilities had kept working. It is the latest in a series of breakdowns in the US that have left many people wondering whether the country was under attack.

Continue readingShow less

The list is long: on Friday the 14th, a systems overload grounded flights across the country for more than four hours; on Monday, a technical problem at the bank JPMorgan Chase delayed transactions; United Airlines had delays because of an internal failure; and, on Tuesday, Apple acknowledged total outages of Apple Music, the App Store and AppleCare after an internal update. There was also a global Microsoft 365 outage, which the company blamed on a technical error.

The Government's response was unanimous. «This is not a cyberattack, nor a coordinated effort by a state», the director of national intelligence, Jay Clayton, told CNBC. The Secretary of State, Marco Rubio, said there was «zero evidence» of involvement by hackers, criminals or another country, although he described the outages as «unsettling». Clayton's explanation: government and companies are updating and patching systems at the same time, and switching old systems off and on again can cause breakdowns.

Democratic senator Mark Warner, the number two on the Senate Intelligence Committee, offered a clue about the rush: artificial intelligence (AI) is allowing attackers and defenders to find security flaws at an unprecedented speed, which forces everything to be fixed more quickly. For Warner, the evidence points to legitimate security work, but the investigation is not over yet. For now, there is no sign that the cases are linked to one another.

Continue readingShow less

How did it happen? On 9 October, Fireworks told Uber that hackers had stolen internal credentials (that is, login details) and that these had been used to reach Uber data. They took billing data and, from some customers, datasets and parts of AI models. Uber cut its data connections to Fireworks and opened an investigation.

Why does this matter? It shows how a single supplier can expose data from several companies. Notion, a note-taking and teamwork app, also confirmed that conversations with its AI assistant, from less than 1% of workspaces, were exposed. Nobody has claimed the attack. Fireworks attributes it to a group that sells stolen access to AI models. It remains to be explained why an AI supplier was holding driving licence data. Drivers have been notified in several states, including California, Illinois and Massachusetts.

04 / Artificial Intelligence 1/3

Reuters investigation: Chinese labs train models on answers from American AI, such as OpenAI's — and Beijing encourages it

Reuters journalistic investigation (300 documents) · the practice is not illegal · US speaks of possible theft of intellectual property · 17/11/2026

Chinese universities and companies are using answers from American artificial intelligence (AI) models, such as OpenAI's, to train their own systems. That is the conclusion of an investigation by the Reuters news agency, published this Tuesday, which analysed more than 300 Chinese documents: scientific papers, patents, company reports, job adverts and official documents. According to the agency, the technique, called «distillation», has gone from academic experiment to common practice in the industry.

Continue readingShow less

How does it work? A large, expensive model, the «teacher», answers questions or solves tasks; those answers are then used to train a smaller model, the «student». One of the examples cited: in November 2024, researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University picked around 5,000 answers from OpenAI's o1 model to continue training a Qwen model, from Alibaba. Researchers at Alibaba and Meituan also described, in papers from 2025 and 2026, using GPT-4 and GPT-4o. Distillation is not illegal and is used all over the world, but OpenAI and Anthropic forbid in their terms the use of their answers to build competing models. Alibaba, Meituan and the university did not respond to Reuters.

Beijing does not order the copying of American models, but it encourages the technique: a package of measures from January, signed by eight state bodies, supports the training and distillation of models for industry, the agency reports. On the American side, an official told Reuters that industrial-scale distillation is the main reason Chinese models have narrowed the gap; two sources linked to Chinese companies dispute this and also point to engineering, data and model design. The issue comes to the table in a tense week: on Monday Washington tightened China's remote access to advanced chips, and Donald Trump and Xi Jinping are meeting in Shenzhen. In July, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned that sanctions and adding companies to the American trade blacklist are «on the table» if distillation turns into theft of intellectual property.

08 / Technology 2/2

Brussels accuses Temu of failing to stop the sale of illegal and dangerous products; fine could reach 6% of worldwide turnover

Preliminary findings of the European Commission (17 Nov) · Temu disagrees and will respond · no fine decided · 17/11/2026

Temu, the Chinese e-commerce platform, is not doing enough to prevent the sale of illegal and unsafe products in the European Union. That is the preliminary finding of the European Commission, released on Tuesday 17 November under the Digital Services Act (DSA), the European rules that require large platforms to control the risks of what is sold and posted on them. If the finding is confirmed, Temu could be fined up to 6% of its annual worldwide turnover and ordered to fix the problem.

Continue readingShow less

Brussels points to three failings. First, checks: Temu only asks for test reports for a small share of products, often does not even check them and does not prioritise physical inspections in categories it itself considers high-risk, such as toys, electronics and cosmetics. Second, moderation: it looks more at the information in listings than at product safety, and the number of human moderators has not kept pace with the growth in listings. Third, sanctions: it penalises shops rather than sellers, who can carry on selling through other shops, even after several serious breaches. «Shopping online should not mean running the risk of buying illegal and unsafe products», said the Commission's Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen.

Temu said it disagrees with the findings, insisted that it invests heavily in compliance and said it will respond, confident of an amicable solution, according to the Eunews website. It is not the company's first problem with Brussels: in May, it was fined €200 million for poorly assessing the platform's risks. The investigation opened in October 2024 also covers other issues, such as the app's potentially addictive design and its recommendation systems, which are not part of these findings.

Serbia: the students who won the election take their seats in Parliament, two years after the Novi Sad tragedy

Serbia's new Parliament met for the first time this Tuesday, 17 November, in Belgrade, and the MPs of the Student List were sworn in. It all began with a tragedy. On 1 November 2024, the canopy of Novi Sad railway station collapsed, killing 16 people. After 1 December, the team will use up the remaining fuel to lower its orbit and switch off all its systems in January 2027.

Continue readingShow less

Why is it ending now? Its systems are ageing and its orbit is drifting, NASA explains. The satellite had already been saving power: MOPITT, the instrument that measured carbon monoxide pollution, was switched off in April 2025, and parts of two other instruments no longer work. Since 2020 Terra has stopped correcting its orbit, to save fuel for a controlled descent.

Terra was the first major satellite in NASA's Earth Observing System. Its five instruments, including MODIS, a camera that photographs the whole planet almost every day, tracked fires, smoke and dust, changes in land use, ice and the make-up of the atmosphere. The 27 years of data remain free and public. NASA advises those who use the data in real time to switch to VIIRS, a similar instrument flying on other US satellites. Its sister satellites are also nearing the end: Aqua is expected to end its science in September 2027 and Aura in August 2028.

Today, 18 November, marks 50 years since the death in Paris of Man Ray, the American artist who helped invent modern photography and passed through Dada and Surrealism without ever settling on a single art form. He was 86. To mark the date, the Magnani-Rocca Foundation, near Parma in Italy, is showing the retrospective «Man Ray: Everything» until 13 December, with more than 250 photographs, paintings, objects, books and films.

Continue readingShow less

His name was Emmanuel Radnitzky and he was born in 1890 in Philadelphia, the son of Jewish immigrants from the Russian Empire: his father was a tailor and his mother a seamstress. He grew up in New York. In 1912, the family changed its surname to Ray because of antisemitism, and «Manny» became «Man». He started out as a painter. In 1915 he met Marcel Duchamp and the two livened up Dada in New York — a movement that mocked the rules of art. In July 1921 he moved to Paris, settled in Montparnasse and became the portraitist of artists and writers, as well as a fashion photographer for magazines such as Vogue and Harper's Bazaar.

Man Ray's style is made of experiments. He rediscovered the photogram by chance — images made without a camera, by placing objects on photographic paper and exposing it to light — and humorously called them «rayographs». With the photographer Lee Miller, he explored solarisation, which reverses some of the tones and creates luminous outlines. He turned everyday objects into art: «Cadeau» is a flat iron with a row of tacks glued to its base. And he made experimental films, such as «Le Retour à la raison» (1923), shot partly without a camera, using the rayograph technique.

A few curiosities. His most famous photograph, «Le Violon d'Ingres» (1924), shows the bare back of Kiki de Montparnasse, his partner in the 1920s, with the two «f-holes» of a violin painted over it. In 2022 it sold at Christie's in New York for $12.4 million, a record for a photograph at auction. When the Nazis occupied Paris in 1940, he fled to Los Angeles, where he devoted himself mainly to painting and married the dancer Juliet Browner, in a double wedding with Max Ernst and Dorothea Tanning. He went back to Paris and stayed there until the end. His grave in Montparnasse reads: «Unconcerned, but not indifferent».

03 / Did you know…?

Did you know that the «Mona Lisa» only became the most famous painting in the world after it was stolen from the Louvre in 1911?

Did you know that, until 1911, Leonardo da Vinci's «Mona Lisa» was not the star of the Louvre in Paris? Critics already admired it, but the general public walked past without much fuss. The 1907 Baedeker guidebook gave it just two sentences — fewer than the Venus de Milo. Everything changed one Monday in August, when the painting vanished from the wall.

Continue readingShow less

The thief was Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian who had worked at the Louvre for the company that put protective glass on the paintings. On the night of 20 August 1911, he hid in a storeroom. The next morning, with the museum closed and wearing the white smock of the staff, he took the painting out of its frame on a service staircase and walked out with it. Nobody noticed it was missing for more than a day: the guards thought it was being photographed. It was only on Tuesday that a painter who had come to copy the work raised the alarm.

The news went round the world. The Louvre closed for about a week and, when it reopened, thousands of people queued to see… the empty wall, with four iron hooks. Among the visitors was the writer Franz Kafka. The police even arrested the poet Guillaume Apollinaire, and Pablo Picasso was questioned, because of another theft of statuettes from the museum; both were cleared. Meanwhile, the face of the Gioconda was spreading across postcards, advertisements, cabaret songs and caricatures.

The painting spent more than two years hidden in a trunk in Paris. In December 1913, Peruggia tried to sell it to a Florence antiques dealer, Alfredo Geri, who alerted the director of the Uffizi Gallery; he was arrested on 11 December. He said he wanted to return the work to Italy — but the painting had been legally bought by King Francis I of France. He spent a few months in prison. The «Mona Lisa» was shown in Florence, Rome and Milan and returned to the Louvre at the beginning of 1914: more than 100,000 people went to see it in the first two days. Today, it receives around eight million visitors a year.
