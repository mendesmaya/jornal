EU summit ends without a deal on the budget to 2034 after almost 15 hours; Spain's Hernández de Cos is the pick for the ECB

No budget deal (fact) · new attempt expected in mid-December · ECB: political choice, to be formalised in December · 26/11/2026

The 27 leaders of the European Union (EU) failed to agree on the long-term budget for 2028–2034. The extraordinary summit in Brussels ended in the early hours of this Friday 27 November, after almost 15 hours of discussion, according to Jornal de Negócios and EUalive. The second day, planned for today, was cancelled. «We did not reach an agreement on the overall size of the budget», admitted the President of the European Council, António Costa, who called the meeting «a necessary stage in the process» and is now looking to the mid-December summit.

On the table was Costa's compromise proposal: around €1.78 trillion, below the almost €1.98 trillion proposed by the European Commission. For some it is too much, for others too little. Seven countries that pay in more than they get back, among them Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden, want bigger cuts and refused to take part in the small-group meetings Costa organised, according to Euronews. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said further cuts will be needed. On the other side, French President Emmanuel Macron called the cuts a «strategic mistake» and warned that, without a deal by 31 December, the negotiations cannot be reopened. Italy's Giorgia Meloni said a deal is «light years» away.

And Portugal? Prime Minister Luís Montenegro said Costa's proposal is «still not enough» and that Portugal will not back it without guarantees for the cohesion funds, aimed at the poorest regions, and for the Azores and Madeira, according to Observador. The proposal gives Portugal €34.9 billion, around €400 million less than earlier versions. The €45 billion loan to Ukraine for 2027 took a back seat: Bulgaria secured an opt-out from the military part, and Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic still refuse to take part, according to EUalive.

There was one decision: the leaders of the euro countries backed Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos, former governor of the Bank of Spain, to succeed Christine Lagarde as president of the European Central Bank (ECB), according to Lusa (Portugal's news agency) and Euronews. Lagarde leaves on 7 February 2027. France's Laurence Boone and Germany's Markus Brunnermeier were put forward for other seats on the bank's board. The appointment still has to be formalised, which is expected to happen in December.

FIFA, the body that runs world football, has been hit by a cyberattack. FIFA says it is working with the authorities, including the police, and condemns the illegal obtaining and publication of the data.

What we know — and what we don't. FIFA did not say when the attack happened, how many people or partners were affected, or what kind of documents were taken. The case comes at a delicate time for the federation. Gianni Infantino, president for 11 years, is standing for a new term running to 2031 in the election set for 18 March, against Japan's Kohzo Tashima and France's Philippe Diallo.

The consequences are serious. ANSSI is trying to establish whether the attackers got in through an IT supplier, which is thought to have been compromised months earlier, and the Paris public prosecutor's office has opened an inquiry.

How long will it last? The French health minister, Stéphanie Rist, spoke of «a few days at the very least, perhaps several weeks», according to Outremers360. The hospital serves around 380,000 people.

Portuguese government grants «national strategic interest» status to Portugal's bid for a European AI gigafactory

Resolution approved · grid connection only if Portugal wins · European call postponed to 30 December · 26/11/2026

The Council of Ministers (Portugal's cabinet) approved on Thursday 26 November a resolution recognising the «national strategic interest» of Portugal's bid for an artificial intelligence (AI) gigafactory — a giant computing centre for training and running AI models. In practice, the government guarantees that the facility will be able to connect to the public electricity grid with a capacity of up to 260 MVA (megavolt-amperes), but only if Portugal wins the European competition.

Continue readingShow less

Why does this matter? The competition is run by EuroHPC, the European joint undertaking for supercomputing, co-funded by the European Commission. The idea is to create up to seven gigafactories in Europe, each with up to 100,000 latest-generation processors. Bidders must prove they can secure enough electricity, and explain the impact of cooling on water and energy consumption.

According to the EFE news agency, the state authorised in July up to €200 million, between 2027 and 2033, to buy computing time; EuroHPC would match that sum in a first phase, and total investment in the Portuguese part would be around €4 billion, with private capital. Preparations are in the hands of the Banco Português de Fomento (Portugal's state-owned development bank), and the government is negotiating with Spain over a joint Iberian bid. No location has been given.

The timetable keeps slipping. The batch in which Portugal is competing had already been moved to early December and, on 25 November, was postponed again to 30 December, according to ECO. European Commissioner Henna Virkkunen called the delays «very regrettable».

Bank of England warns: the money borrowed for the AI race is increasingly «complex, circular and opaque»

Official Bank of England assessment · the risks described are warnings, not crisis forecasts · 26/11/2026

The Bank of England thinks the way artificial intelligence (AI) is being financed could become a problem for the financial system. In its financial stability report, published on Thursday 26 November, it describes the financing of data centres as «increasingly complex, circular and opaque», which increases the links between companies and markets and makes contagion easier if something goes wrong.

Continue readingShow less

At the centre are the so-called «hyperscalers», the small group of tech giants building the big data centres. According to Reuters, they will issue more than $305 billion in debt this year, more than most governments, including the British one. According to Bloomberg, the central bank estimates these companies' total exposure to AI at more than $4 trillion, also counting commitments that sit off their balance sheets. Those commitments could come under pressure if expectations of AI profits are revised down, the bank warns.

Even after recent falls, stock market valuations remain «stretched», the report says. The bank also urges banks not to depend on a single cloud provider, after recent outages at Microsoft and Google, and wants them to switch to cryptography that can resist future quantum computers: priority systems by the end of 2031, the rest by 2035.

Did you know that the first people ever photographed were a shoeshiner and his customer, on a Paris street that looked deserted? This autumn, they got a plaque

In 1838, Louis Daguerre pointed his camera from his studio window at the Boulevard du Temple, in Paris. The street was full of people and carts, but in the picture it looks empty. Only two figures remained: a man having his shoes shined and the shoeshiner. They are, as far as is known, the first people to appear in a photograph.

Continue readingShow less

Why only them? Because of the exposure time. The daguerreotype, the technique Daguerre presented to the world in 1839, needed several minutes of light to fix the image on a metal plate. Anyone walking past did not stay in the same spot long enough to leave a mark. The customer, standing with his leg propped up, and the shoeshiner, bent over the shoes, kept still enough to go down in history — without ever knowing it.

The original plate no longer exists as it was. It was given to King Ludwig I of Bavaria and was badly damaged in the bombing of the Second World War. The version that circulates today comes mainly from a copy made in 1937 by Beaumont Newhall, then at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. And Daguerre's studio, next to his theatre of illusions, the Diorama, burned down in 1839.

The most recent part of the story dates from this autumn. Three Swedish filmmakers — Maximilien Van Aertryck, Axel Danielson and Kalle Boman — spent two years in archives, maps and tax records, and commissioned 3D models of the 1838 neighbourhood. Two teams of architects arrived, each on its own, at points a few steps from each other. The spot is next to today's Place de la République, and in October Paris City Council placed a bronze plaque there called «Un détail imprévu» — «an unforeseen detail». Next time you go to Paris, you'll know where to look.

Since then, Stop has gone from problem to heritage: it was listed as a monument of municipal interest in July 2025, and the council began a bid to have its musical use recognised as national intangible cultural heritage, in a study by the University of Porto and Lusíada University.

Not everything is simple. According to a JN report in August, rents have gone up — some rooms that cost 100 to 150 euros a month now cost 250, plus electricity — and many bands already share a room. Some fear gentrification. But the community keeps moving: this summer, nine Stop bands gave free concerts in the city's bandstands, in a series organised by the musicians' association with the municipal company Ágora. And the former cinemas, with around 240 and 120 seats, could become the stage that was missing, if the deal and the safety measures are finalised.
