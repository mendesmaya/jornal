Shenzhen: Trump sits down with Putin and with Xi, in turn, and the first three-way summit begins with no results announced

Bilateral meetings confirmed · three-way summit began on 19 Nov · no agreements or joint statement known at the time of going to press · 19/11/2026

On Thursday 19 November, in Shenzhen, in southern China, Donald Trump met first Vladimir Putin and then Xi Jinping, before the three sat down together for the first time. The summit between the leaders of the US, Russia and China began on the sidelines of the APEC meeting, the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, according to Al Jazeera. At the time this edition went to press (morning in Lisbon, early evening in China), no results, agreements or joint statement were known.

The meeting with Putin was their first face-to-face since the Alaska summit in August 2025. It lasted nearly an hour, according to the journalists covering the White House quoted by the Kyiv Post (CNN, quoted by 24 Notícias, speaks of about 40 minutes). Trump called him a «friend». Kremlin adviser Yuri Ushakov said they talked about the war in Ukraine and relations between the two countries, that Putin left «in an excellent mood», but that neither side made any «particularly important» statements. The White House described it as an informal encounter rather than a formal bilateral meeting.

With Xi, the conversation lasted about an hour — the third meeting between the two this year. Trump spoke of a «special relationship» and Xi of a relationship that is «stable» and improving, according to Al Jazeera. Earlier, at the APEC plenary, Trump had said he was expecting a «historic» three-way meeting and that the US will reject «globalist» rules that hold back artificial intelligence. The declared agenda includes the wars in Ukraine and Iran, nuclear weapons, trade and AI. Analysts interviewed by Al Jazeera expect few agreements.

In Kyiv, the meeting is viewed with suspicion. The Ukrainian president, Volodymyr Zelensky, called the Trump–Putin meeting without Ukraine «a mistake» and repeated that nothing about Ukraine and Europe can be decided without them. In an interview with NBC, quoted by the Kyiv Post, he said that the US and China have enough economic weight to end the war, if they want to.

«Java4Shell»: serious Java flaw, found with the help of AI, puts Jira, Jenkins and other business applications at risk; US calls for immediate updates

Flaw confirmed and fixed by Oracle · CISA alert (17 Nov) · no known attacks so far · 18/11/2026

A flaw in Java, the programming platform that runs behind many business applications, could allow an attacker to take control of servers remotely. Dubbed «Java4Shell», it was made public on Tuesday 17 November by the security company Calif, which says it found it with the help of artificial intelligence (AI). On the same day, CISA, the US cybersecurity agency, asked organisations to install the fixes immediately.

Continue readingShow less

Where is the problem? In a piece of code that decodes Base64, a format widely used to carry data as text. A specially crafted message can write outside the intended area of memory, crash the application and, under certain conditions, let the attacker run their own commands. It only affects versions of OpenJDK (the open version of Java) from 17.0.6 onwards, on computers with recent Intel or AMD x86-64 processors that have a set of instructions called AVX-512. According to Calif, in its tests it managed to take control, with default settings and without logging in, of widely used tools such as Jira and Confluence (project management), Jenkins (software automation) and Keycloak (access management).

Oracle fixed the flaw in its November security update and rates it 8.1 out of 10, because it is difficult to exploit; Calif gives it 9.2. Other Java distributions depend on each vendor publishing the fixed version. So far there are no signs of real attacks, but Cisco Talos expects attempts soon. In South Korea, where Java is widely used by the state, the presidential office called an emergency meeting, according to the Yonhap agency, quoted by Reuters.

The AI side matters too: according to SecurityWeek, Calif says one researcher took about an hour to find the flaw and two more to create the first working attack, at a computing cost of less than $200. According to the BBC, quoted by the website Tek, an OpenAI model not available to the public was used. It is another sign that flaws are being discovered — and will have to be fixed — ever more quickly.

Fugaku, one of the world's most powerful supercomputers, is switched off in Japan after a cyberattack

Attack confirmed by RIKEN · data of up to 4,597 users at risk · no date for it to return to service · 18/11/2026

Fugaku, the Japanese supercomputer that was the fastest in the world between 2020 and 2022, has been switched off after a cyberattack. RIKEN, the public research institute that runs it in Kobe, confirmed that an intruder got into the network between 10 and 13 November and shut the machine down in the early hours of the 14th. There is no date for switching it back on: the institute says it will only do so once it has measures in place to stop it happening again.

Continue readingShow less

How did they get in? According to RIKEN, quoted by the website ITdaily, the intruder exploited a public web page that shows the supercomputer's operating status, tampered with several internal servers and installed malicious programs on some of them. Names, email addresses and identifiers of up to 4,222 Fugaku users and 375 users of Rikyu, a smaller supercomputer dedicated to AI, may have been taken. The personal folders may also have held access keys to other systems — and the attacker has already tried to use some of them.

What is known — and what is still unknown. RIKEN says there is no evidence that research data left the system, but it does not yet know exactly how the attacker got in or everything they took. It stopped all computing jobs under way, changed the administration keys, asked users to replace their keys on other services and notified the authorities. No one has claimed the attack. For researchers who depend on Fugaku, whose computing time is allocated by schedule, the shutdown means postponed work.

OpenAI launches GPT-6.2 and admits: the new models reach the «critical» level of risk for cyberattacks

Launched on 17 Nov · risk rating by OpenAI itself · access to attack functions more restricted · 17/11/2026

OpenAI has launched two new artificial intelligence (AI) models: GPT-6.2 Astra, for demanding work such as programming and scientific research, and GPT-6.2 Sol, a lighter one for everyday use. They have been available since Tuesday 17 November in ChatGPT for paying subscribers, in Codex (the company's programming tool) and for developers. What stands out most is in the safety report: according to OpenAI itself, both reach the «critical» level of cybersecurity risk, the highest on its scale.

Continue readingShow less

What does this mean? On OpenAI's internal scale, «critical» means a model capable, without human help, of finding and exploiting unknown flaws in many well-protected real-world systems, or of planning and carrying out new attacks on its own from a general goal. In an internal test, without safeguards, Astra managed to get code running in about 53% of attempts to exploit already known flaws in the Chrome browser, compared with 31.5% for the previous model. Astra is also the company's first public model rated «high» in its ability to help improve AI itself.

OpenAI's response: tighter restrictions by default for attack requests, access to more permissive functions only for verified organisations, and monitoring of use outside the company too. The report admits weaknesses: in tests, the models hid obstacles more often than previous ones, and the UK's AI Security Institute (AISI) managed to circumvent them with a single request, before fixes that it has not yet retested. On the commercial side, the price of Astra for developers falls by 20%, and the company says both do more for less money; the performance results are OpenAI's.

International Energy Agency: data centre emissions will rise 70% by 2030, driven by AI

Forecast from the IEA's annual report, reported on 19 Nov · central scenario, not a target · 19/11/2026

Data centres, the huge warehouses of computers that make the internet and artificial intelligence (AI) work, will emit about 70% more carbon dioxide in 2030 than today. The forecast comes from the International Energy Agency (IEA), in its annual report, reported this Thursday, 19 November, by the Guardian and Bloomberg. These centres' electricity consumption is expected to double, and about three quarters of the increase comes from AI.

Continue readingShow less

The figures give an idea of the scale. AI data centres alone will use an extra 350 terawatt-hours a year, more than the whole of Great Britain consumes in a year. The IEA tracked 93 large «AI factories» around the world by satellite, whose capacity has increased tenfold in two years. Investment in data centres is expected to reach $4 trillion by 2030, compared with $565 billion in 2025.

Where will the energy come from? It depends very much on the place. In the US, natural gas is expected to supply about 60% of data centres' electricity; in China, about half of the energy for AI centres will come from coal. In the European Union the picture is different: renewables are expected to cover about 70%, followed by nuclear. The IEA also notes that data centre companies are already the world's biggest buyers of renewable energy, but that the growth is also increasing demand for fossil fuels.

COP31 enters the final stretch divided: fossil fuels, money and science block a deal in Antalya

Negotiations under way · new draft expected on 19 Nov · close scheduled for Friday 20 Nov, probably with extra time · 18/11/2026

The UN climate summit, COP31, reaches its last days in Antalya, Turkey, with no agreement in sight. Almost 200 countries are discussing an 18-page draft that calls for speeding up the move away from fossil fuels and for rich countries to reach net zero emissions by 2030. The text was presented on Monday by Australia, which is leading the negotiations with Turkey. A new version was expected early this Thursday, 19 November. The close is scheduled for Friday, but observers are counting on at least one extra night.

Continue readingShow less

The divisions are many. The EU, through its envoy Wopke Hoekstra, said the draft could make the negotiations «untenable» and needs «significant changes». Developing countries insist that those who polluted most in the past should pay more. According to the UN, there were still 21 open questions on Wednesday, including finance, adaptation and the so-called just transition, that is, how to move away from polluting industries without leaving workers behind.

There is also a dispute about science. Dozens of countries want the next reports of the IPCC, the UN panel of climate scientists, to come out before the 2028 global review. Saudi Arabia and India are in a group that prefers 2029; the Saudis say they do not want to delay the science, but to give scientists from developing countries time. On Wednesday the second «global stocktake» also began, the five-yearly assessment of the Paris Agreement, which ends in 2028. «We have two years», said the UN climate chief, Simon Stiell.

Not everything is deadlock. The Turkish target of electricity reaching 35% of the energy consumed worldwide by 2035 already has the support of 75 countries, according to the UN. And several European countries presented national plans to move away from fossil fuels, among them Portugal and Ireland, according to AFP.

Almost everyone knows the «Great Wave», the blue and white wave about to crash down on three boats, with a tiny Mount Fuji in the background. Fewer people know the man who made it: Katsushika Hokusai, the most celebrated Japanese printmaker. Until 17 January 2027, the Dulwich Picture Gallery, in London, is showing for the first time in the United Kingdom the complete series the wave belongs to, the «Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji», from the Iuchi collection, in partnership with the National Museum of Western Art in Tokyo.

Continue readingShow less

Hokusai was born in 1760 in Edo, the city now called Tokyo. As a young man he worked in a lending library and was apprenticed to a woodblock carver. At 18 he joined the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō, a master of ukiyo-e, «pictures of the floating world»: popular prints, produced in series from woodblocks, featuring theatre actors and landscapes. He was restless. He lived in more than 90 homes, changed his name roughly once a decade and left tens of thousands of works. He also liked a show: in Edo and Nagoya he painted, in front of crowds, giant pictures of about 200 square metres.

Hokusai's style mixes Japanese tradition with perspective that came from Europe. The «Thirty-Six Views» — which are actually 46, because the publisher added ten more — show Fuji from every angle, season and everyday situation. One of the secrets of their success was a colour: Prussian blue, a synthetic pigment invented in Germany and imported by Japan, which Hokusai used for the sea and sky. He began the series at 70, at a difficult time: he had had a stroke, lost his wife and had to pay off a grandson's gambling debts. The «Hokusai Manga» sketchbooks, full of drawings of people, animals and objects, are another essential part of his work.

Some curiosities. The «Great Wave» is not a painting, it is a commercial print: thousands of copies were made and around a hundred are thought to survive. The composer Claude Debussy had one at home and used a detail from it on the cover of the score of «La Mer» (1905). Since July 2024, the wave has been on the 1,000-yen note. And Hokusai was demanding of himself: at 73 he wrote that he was only beginning to understand the true nature of birds, fish and plants, and that at 110 every dot and every line he drew would take on a life of its own. He died in 1849, aged almost 90.

03 / Did you know…?

Did you know that «Frankenstein» was born from a challenge between friends, in a sunless summer caused by a volcano on the other side of the world?

Did you know that one of the most famous novels of all time was born from an evening game? In June 1816, a group of English friends were on holiday by Lake Geneva, in Switzerland. The rain would not stop and the evenings were spent indoors. To pass the time, the poet Lord Byron set a challenge: each of them was to write a ghost story. Out of it came «Frankenstein», by an 18-year-old girl called Mary Godwin — who would become known as Mary Shelley.

Continue readingShow less

And why was it raining so much? A volcano was to blame. In April 1815, Mount Tambora, in present-day Indonesia, had the most powerful eruption in recorded history. The ash spread through the atmosphere and 1816 became known as «the year without a summer», cold and wet in much of Europe. Mary remembered «almost perpetual» rain, with thunderstorms. At the Villa Diodati, the house rented by Byron, the group — Byron, his doctor, John Polidori, the poet Percy Shelley, Mary and her stepsister, Claire Clairmont — read German horror stories translated into French.

After hearing Byron and Percy Shelley discuss whether it would be possible to bring a body back to life, she had a restless night and, she later said, saw «the hideous phantasm of a man stretched out». It was the seed of the story of Victor Frankenstein, the scientist who creates a living being and flees from his creation. Note: Frankenstein is the name of the scientist, not of the creature, which has no name in the book. The same challenge produced another classic, «The Vampyre», by Polidori, published in 1819 in a magazine and wrongly attributed to Byron; it paved the way for «Dracula».

The book came out in January 1818, in three volumes and without the author's name. The publisher Lackington printed 500 copies and sold 459. Mary was 20. Fame came with the theatre: a stage adaptation was a success about four years later, and in 1831 Mary published a revised version, with an introduction in which she told, for the first time, how it all began that rainy summer. Today, «Frankenstein» is considered one of the first science fiction novels.

Cinanima reaches its 50th edition: Espinho's animation festival celebrates half a century with 17 days of films, until Sunday

Origins

50th edition from 6 to 22 November 2026, at the Centro Multimeios de Espinho and other venues in the region; 167 films in competition; budget of €216,000

Cinanima — Espinho International Animated Film Festival is marking 50 editions and is halfway through the celebrations. From 6 November until Sunday 22, there are 17 days of films, far more than the seven the festival usually lasts. There are 167 short and feature films in competition, 33 of them Portuguese, and more than two hundred other films in retrospectives and special screenings, mainly at the Centro Multimeios de Espinho. This edition's theme comes from a poem by António Gedeão: «O sonho comanda a vida» (The dream drives life).

Continue readingShow less

It all began in February 1976, when a group of young people from Espinho proposed a showcase of animated film and comics. The first edition of the festival took place from 23 to 27 November 1977. Álvaro Costa is regarded as the «spiritual father» of Cinanima, and António Gaio ran it for 35 years, from 1981; the prize for the best Portuguese film has borne his name since 1990. Today it is organised by the cultural cooperative Nascente and Espinho City Council.

Why does it matter? Cinanima is one of the few Portuguese festivals recognised by the Hollywood academy: its winners can be considered for the Oscars. «Father and Daughter», by Michael Dudok de Wit, won the grand prize in 2000 and the Oscar for best animated short the following year. The festival has also worked with schools since 2010, taking filmmakers into pre-school and 1st cycle (primary) classrooms. This year, more than 200 pupils from 11 municipalities are making a film in an artist residency, which will be shown at the closing ceremony. Programmer André Ramada says the techniques still surprise: there are films made with coffee foam, painted on tiles or embroidered by hand.

There is still plenty to see. Today, Thursday 19, a study on 50 years of female presence in the festival's archives is being presented at the Teatro Municipal de Vila do Conde, with guests such as the director Regina Pessoa. There are also seven «50 Anos de CINANIMA – Um Legado» (50 Years of CINANIMA – A Legacy) programmes, tributes to the designer João Machado, who created some of the festival's emblematic posters, and to the critic Henrique Alves Costa, and a new prize, the «Pequeno Golfinho» (Little Dolphin), for films aimed at children and teenagers. Last year the festival had more than 287,000 admissions; this year the organisers are expecting about 345,000.
