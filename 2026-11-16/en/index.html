Beijing confirms the first three-way summit between Xi, Trump and Putin, on Thursday in Shenzhen; nuclear weapons and Iran on the agenda, Ukraine not

Meeting confirmed by Beijing, Washington and Moscow · agenda not officially disclosed; no agreement expected · 16/11/2026

China's Foreign Ministry confirmed this Monday, 16 November, that Chinese President Xi Jinping will meet US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin on Thursday 19 November in Shenzhen, in southern China, on the sidelines of the APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) summit, which takes place on 18 and 19 November. It will be the first three-way meeting of the leaders of the three biggest nuclear powers. The White House had announced the meeting on Friday and the Kremlin confirmed the date on Saturday; Beijing was the only capital that had not yet done so.

The Chinese statement does not say what will be discussed. On the US side, the White House says Trump mainly wants to talk about reducing nuclear arsenals — the New START treaty, which limited US and Russian strategic weapons, expired in February, and Trump wants a new agreement that includes China. Beijing has said it is not “fair, reasonable or realistic” to ask it to join those negotiations and that the US and Russia must first reduce their own arsenals. According to US officials cited by AFP, Trump will also talk about the war with Iran, but not about the war in Ukraine; a senior administration official said they did not expect “any kind of agreement”.

Before the three-way meeting, Trump is expected to hold a bilateral meeting and a working lunch with Xi on Thursday, according to TRT. A separate meeting between Trump and Putin has not been agreed, said Kremlin adviser Yuri Ushakov. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called the meeting “extremely significant”, but warned that whatever is agreed cannot be imposed on other countries; Russian MP Pyotr Tolstoy went as far as speaking of a “new Yalta”, a reference to the 1945 conference that divided post-war Europe. German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier told the broadcaster ARD that Xi had told him the war in Ukraine is “a European conflict” in which Europeans must also have a seat at the table.

Attack on La Poste subsidiary exposes names and addresses of 22 million people, roughly one in three residents of France

Fact confirmed by Maileva and La Poste · perpetrators and method of entry yet to be established; judicial investigation under way · 14/11/2026

Maileva, a company in the French La Poste group that prints and sends mail (invoices, payslips, registered letters, notices to attend) on behalf of thousands of businesses, has confirmed that a cyberattack led to the theft of the first names, surnames and addresses of 22 million people. The figure was confirmed by the company to the specialist website Cyberattaque.org and announced publicly by La Poste on Saturday 14 November. Most of the victims are not direct customers: they are the recipients of letters sent by the thousands of companies that use the service.

Continue readingShow less

The intrusion dates back to mid-September. On 23 September, Maileva suspended its services after detecting suspicious activity and only resumed them six days later; the day after the suspension, it admitted attempts at unauthorised access to customer accounts to send fraudulent mail. According to Cyberattaque.org, internal documents pointed to around 39 million mail records, corresponding to 22 million different people. The company says it has warned all the organisations affected, informed the CNIL (the French data protection authority) and filed a complaint; it is up to each client company to notify the people whose data appeared in its mail.

The data confirmed by Maileva is limited to names and addresses, but some clients have reported more. Foch Hospital, on the outskirts of Paris, warned that payment reminders sent between 9 and 23 September, with invoice number, treatment dates and amounts owed, may have been seen, without information on the care provided or bank details. A social housing company in northern France said letters up to ten years old may be involved. It has not been disclosed how the attackers got in or who they are. The hospital warns of the risk of scam attempts by email, text message, post or phone in the name of well-known organisations.

03 / Cybersecurity 3/3

FBI warns that ARTEX, an attack tool built on AI agents, is being used in intrusions that take control of networks in under three hours

Official FBI warning (13 Nov) · tool used by attackers since August; victims and perpetrators not fully identified · 13/11/2026

On Friday 13 November the FBI published an urgent alert (FLASH) about ARTEX, an open-source penetration-testing program that uses artificial intelligence agents and has been exploited by hackers since at least August. According to the alert, cited by GBHackers and Cyber Press, some intrusions went from initial reconnaissance to administrator-level access in under three hours, and investigators found more than 1,600 consoles of the tool exposed on the internet in early November.

Continue readingShow less

ARTEX turns goals written in everyday language (“get into this network”, for example) into commands carried out by several automated agents, with little human intervention. It has no AI model of its own: it connects to language models from various companies, and attackers mainly use cheaper Chinese models, according to the alert. It was published in July by a developer identified only by the username “Autumn-27”, who removed it from GitHub on 8 October after reports of abuse, but copies and a commercial version remain available.

Among the cases described are a campaign against South Korean financial institutions, with the theft of the personal data of around 67,000 people, and the compromise of ConnectWise's cloud backup service, which exposed more than 2,250 customers. Targeted sectors include finance, public administration, defence, health, energy and telecommunications. The FBI recommends taking an inventory of internet-facing systems, restricting access to cloud administration consoles and monitoring unexpected connections to AI model services from internal networks.

06 / Artificial Intelligence 3/3

Google's teams of AI agents verify famous proofs in formal language, from the Weil conjectures to Hilbert's tenth problem

Results announced by Google · no independent assessment; some of the tools are not yet available to the public · 13/11/2026

On Friday 13 November Google Research released results obtained with /teamwork, a feature of its Antigravity programming environment that puts several artificial intelligence agents to work in parallel and checks what they produce with automated verifiers. The most striking case is mathematics: the system wrote around 1.8 million lines in Lean, a language in which a program checks every step of a proof, to formalise the Weil conjectures, proved by Pierre Deligne in 1974, in work that required 28,800 calls to sub-agents.

Continue readingShow less

According to the company, the system also formalised for the first time a recent proof of Hilbert's tenth problem for rings of integers (around 919,000 lines), the Birkhoff conjecture and the Bochner–Riesz conjecture in three dimensions, and helped researchers from Google and from universities in Hong Kong and the US to prove the Courtade–Kumar conjecture, an information theory problem open since 2013. Outside mathematics, Google says it converted around 170,000 lines of a video decoder from C++ to Rust, making it 20% to 30% faster, and identified molecular structures from nuclear magnetic resonance data with 95% accuracy at the first attempt.

The results are described by Google itself, which acknowledges that human experts checked and edited many of them and that it is up to mathematicians to confirm that the formal statements match the original problems. The company has opened applications for 10,000 academic researchers to get six months' free access to the highest tier of the service; some features will only arrive later.

The US Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs, Jacob Helberg, said the partners now treat the security of these technologies as a shared responsibility. No locations, funding or deadlines were disclosed.

The agreement comes days before a planned meeting between Donald Trump and Xi Jinping at the APEC summit in Shenzhen, and in the midst of the controversy over Nvidia chips diverted to China. On the European side, member states authorised the Commission to negotiate on 11 November, but, according to Poland Insight, there are still reservations about the AI provisions and the scope of the obligations.

03 / Did you know…?

Did you know that Times New Roman, the most widely used typeface in the world, was born because a typographer thought The Times newspaper was badly printed — and that the paper itself dropped it in 1972?

Times New Roman, the typeface that for years was the default in Microsoft Word and still fills school assignments, theses and official documents today, was born from a newspaper's commission: the London daily The Times first used it on 3 October 1932, and it was the first typeface designed by a newspaper for its own use.

Continue readingShow less

It all began with a criticism. The typographer Stanley Morison, an adviser to the Monotype company, told The Times that the paper was behind the times in modern typography, and the management challenged him to do better. With the draughtsman Victor Lardent, Morison started from older typefaces, such as Plantin, inspired by the types of the 16th-century French engraver Robert Granjon, and refined them for newsprint: he increased the height of the lower-case letters, set the letters closer together to fit more text on each line and save paper and ink, and strengthened the contrast between thick and thin strokes. The proofs were tested by eyesight specialists and read for hours in natural and artificial light; the name distinguished it from the paper's previous typeface, “Times Old Roman”.

The newspaper had exclusive use of it for a year. After that, Times spread around the world — under two names: Monotype called it Times New Roman and Linotype Times Roman, and since Microsoft licensed the first company's catalogue and Apple the second's, the name still differs between PC and Mac. Ironically, The Times swapped it in 1972 for Walter Tracy's Times Europa, and Microsoft dethroned it in Word in 2007 with Calibri, which in turn gave way to Aptos in 2023.

The typeface is still a talking point. In 2023, the US State Department replaced Times New Roman with Calibri, as it was considered easier to read for people with visual impairments; in December 2025, Secretary of State Marco Rubio ordered a return to Times New Roman, in 14-point size, as being “more formal and professional”, describing the previous change as a “wasteful” diversity measure. Accessibility researchers point out that serifs — the small strokes at the ends of letters — can make reading harder for people with dyslexia or low vision. Ninety-four years on, a design made to save newsprint is still a matter of political debate.
