Portugal's 2027 State Budget passes thanks to the PS abstaining, but the opposition's changes lead the Government to forecast a 0.2% deficit

Approved in the final overall vote on 24 Nov · goes to the President of the Republic · Government promises to correct the deficit over the year · 24/11/2026

The country now has a Budget for 2027. Luís Montenegro's government's proposal was approved on Tuesday 24 November in the final overall vote in the Assembleia da República, Portugal's parliament. Only the PSD and CDS-PP voted in favour. The PS (Socialists) and JPP abstained, which allowed it to pass. Chega, IL, Livre, PCP, BE and PAN voted against. PAN switched from abstaining to voting against because of an approved proposal that obliges RTP, the public broadcaster, to show bullfights, according to RTP.

The document leaving Parliament is not the one that went in. In the detailed stage, in which amendments are voted on article by article, 273 opposition proposals were approved, 108 of them from the PS, according to ECO. Among them are zero VAT for three months on a basket of essential foods, a freeze on university tuition fees, more allowances for the PSP and the GNR (Portugal's police forces) and a requirement that any increase in fuel tax (ISP) must go through Parliament. In other words: the Government lost several votes to the PS and Chega, which together have a majority.

Why does this matter? According to the Ministry of Finance's figures, submitted on Tuesday itself, the changes are worth around €800 million. Instead of a small surplus of 0.1% of GDP (€275 million), the State would run a deficit of 0.2% (€525 million). Montenegro accused the PS and Chega of disloyalty, insisted that «we will carry on governing» and that the Government «will do everything to avoid a political and constitutional crisis». He also said there will be no deficit because the Executive will work during the year to correct what it considers to be mistakes. The PS leader, José Luís Carneiro, called it «a bad budget» and explained that the party abstained to avoid a political crisis. André Ventura, of Chega, described it as a budget «negotiated with the PS».

On the same day, the European Commission found that Portugal's budget plan complies with EU rules, but warned that spending could grow well above what is recommended. Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis acknowledged that, in theory, a 0.2% deficit still fits the criterion of accounts «close to balance». The Council of the EU is expected to adopt on 11 December a recommendation allowing Portugal to deviate from the spending growth limits, according to Forbes Portugal. The Budget now goes to the President of the Republic, António José Seguro, who before the vote had said he hoped the country would have «an approved Budget», according to RTP.

Hackers using AI tools broke into the servers that run Check Point «firewalls» at at least 22 organisations

Campaign described by GreyNoise · attacker unknown · Check Point says the flaw used had already been fixed · attacks may be continuing · 24/11/2026

A group of hackers whose name is still unknown broke into the servers that control Check Point «firewalls» at at least 22 organisations in several countries, and is believed to have used artificial intelligence (AI) tools to automate much of the work. The case was revealed on Tuesday 24 November by the American security company GreyNoise, according to SecurityWeek, Cybersecurity Dive and The Hacker News. The victims are in energy, finance, public administration, transport, mining, engineering and manufacturing.

Continue readingShow less

Why does this matter? These servers, called Security Management, are the control centre for a company's «firewalls»: this is where the rules on what comes into and goes out of the network are set. According to The Hacker News, the attacker used a flaw in Check Point's management software (CVE-2026-16232), since fixed, to launch more than a hundred tasks on those servers. These included stealing the «firewall» databases and the files used to issue internal digital certificates, creating a new certificate and switching off security policies. Data was taken from the 24 compromised servers, according to SecurityWeek.

Where does AI come in? GreyNoise, which has given the group the codename GT13, believes the attacker uses AI «agents», programs that look for vulnerable systems and attack them automatically, with only occasional human involvement. The researchers counted 99 internet addresses, more than 1,400 connections and more than 800 commands. They also saw attempts to exploit old flaws in products from Citrix, Ivanti, VMware, Microsoft Exchange and Windows. This is a suspicion on the researchers' part, not a proven conclusion. GreyNoise does not know how the attacker first got into the servers, and has not been able to link it to a country or a known group.

What we know — and what we don't. GreyNoise tracked the activity between 17 October and 2 November and thinks the campaign is still going on, because the attacker kept access to the systems. The victims are in around 16 countries, mainly in Europe and Asia-Pacific, as well as the US, according to The Hacker News; the countries named by SecurityWeek include the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Austria and the Netherlands. Check Point, quoted by The Hacker News, says this is not a new technique but a flaw that had already been disclosed and had a fix available, and urges customers to update and to restrict access to their management servers.

02 / Cybersecurity 2/3

Drupal flaw found by Anthropic's automatic scanner is already being used in attacks; US orders it fixed by Friday

Attacks confirmed by the Drupal team and by CISA · fix available since 18 Nov · number of sites attacked unknown · 24/11/2026

A serious flaw in Drupal, one of the most widely used programs for building and running websites, is already being exploited by hackers. Drupal's security team confirmed attacks on real sites since 19 November, a day after it published the fix. On Tuesday 24 November, CISA, the US cybersecurity agency, added the flaw (CVE-2026-132165) to its list of exploited vulnerabilities and gave federal agencies until Friday 27 to fix it, according to SecurityWeek.

Continue readingShow less

How does it work? The problem lies in the installer that comes with Drupal and stays on the server after the site has been set up. In certain configurations, an attacker without an account can use it to reinstall the site, take control of it or run their own code on the server. The Drupal team raised the rating to «highly critical» (20 out of 25) when it learned of the attacks. The fixed versions are 11.4.13, 11.3.22 and 10.6.22; versions that are no longer supported will not get a fix.

Who found it? Anthropic's OSS Scanner, a free, automatic service launched in October that looks for flaws in registered open-source projects and alerts the people in charge. It is the third Drupal flaw exploited in attacks this year, according to SecurityWeek.

What should you do? Update. Drupal also recommends blocking access to the installer even after updating. Some attack paths are closed off if the server cannot change the «settings.php» configuration file. It is not known how many sites have been attacked, or by whom.

OpenAI says GPT-6 can now design new proteins, and some of them worked in the lab

Results released by OpenAI in a study not yet peer-reviewed · tests carried out with a partner · independent confirmation still lacking · 24/11/2026

OpenAI's language models are learning to design proteins. On Tuesday 24 November the company released a study in which it says the GPT-6 family can create new protein structures and sequences just by reasoning and writing code, without a specialised biology program doing the design. Some of these proteins were made and tested in the lab by partner company Onava, according to Let's Data Science and N Globe. The study has not yet been reviewed by other scientists.

Continue readingShow less

What was tested? Small proteins called «minibinders», designed to latch on to a specific target, such as the molecules PD-L1 and VEGF-A, which are linked to cancer and inflammatory diseases. GPT-6.2 was given the shape of five target proteins and proposed candidates. Of the 322 samples tested, 35 showed signs of binding to the target: around 11%. In another test, the model had to write the sequence of already known proteins without seeing the original. For the protein MDM2, 75 of 88 proposals worked, even though many were quite different from the original sequence. For OpenAI, this shows the model is not simply repeating what it has memorised.

Why does this matter? Designing proteins to order is one of the routes to new medicines and vaccines. Until now, the work has depended on tools built solely for that purpose. If a general-purpose model can do it, researchers could switch tasks with written instructions alone, without training a new program. OpenAI says its latest models matched or beat the specialised tools in most of 14 computer-based tests.

What we know — and what we don't. The specialised tools have not gone away: they still suggested sequences and filtered candidates. The model is also still weak at predicting the full shape of a protein. And binding to a target in an assay is not the same as treating a disease: the study includes no potency or toxicity data, N Globe notes. The figures are OpenAI's own and have not yet been reproduced by others.

Tidal will delete around 27 million songs made entirely by AI and stop accepting new ones from 9 December

Decision announced by Tidal · removals begin on 9 December · music made with the help of AI still allowed · 24/11/2026

The music platform Tidal is banning songs generated entirely by artificial intelligence (AI). The company announced on Tuesday 24 November that from 9 December it will start deleting these tracks from its catalogue and turning away new ones, according to TechCrunch and Digital Music News. That is around 27 million songs, 6.3% of everything Tidal has. According to the company, 96% of them have never been played even once.

Continue readingShow less

Why now? Because the flood has not stopped. Over the past 90 days, 43% of tracks uploaded to Tidal were detected as 100% AI-generated. On 6 November, more than 60% of the day's deliveries were entirely synthetic, says Digital Music News. In June, Tidal had already stopped paying royalties on these songs and started labelling them, but that was not enough. «Something more drastic is needed», Tidal's head of music, Robert Anderson, wrote on X, quoted by TechCrunch.

How will it work? Tidal will use audio-detection technology from the French company Ircam Amplify and check the information that comes with each upload. Distributors will no longer be able to deliver tracks made solely by AI. Those who use AI as a production tool, alongside human work, can still publish. The company expects to turn away between 80,000 and 85,000 tracks a day.

According to several sources, Tidal is the first major streaming service to go this far. The Bandcamp store has already banned AI-made music; Deezer removes these tracks from recommendations and editorial playlists; Spotify and YouTube Music prefer to identify them with labels.

A US federal jury has found Huawei guilty of conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank fraud and sanctions violations. What was it accused of? Born and died

The Museu Municipal de Tavira (Tavira Municipal Museum), in the Algarve, is showing more than a hundred photographs by Fernando Lemos (1926–2019), one of the central names of Portuguese surrealism, until 3 April. The exhibition «As imagens que nos olham» (The images that look at us) opened on 14 November at the Palácio da Galeria and is part of the celebrations of the centenary of the artist's birth. The photographs, almost all from 1949 to 1952, come from the Fundação Cupertino de Miranda, in Vila Nova de Famalicão, and the exhibition is curated by Marlene Oliveira.

Continue readingShow less

Fernando Lemos was born in Lisbon on 3 May 1926. He studied at the Escola António Arroio and then painting at the Sociedade Nacional de Belas Artes, and worked in industrial lithography. In 1949 he took part in the first exhibition of the Lisbon Surrealist Group and, in the following years, photographed writers and artists who were his friends, such as Sophia de Mello Breyner Andresen, Alexandre O'Neill, Mário Cesariny and Jorge de Sena. In 1952 he exhibited at the former Casa Jalco, on Rua Ivens, with Marcelino Vespeira and Fernando de Azevedo: painting, drawing, objects, collages and photography. Shortly afterwards, fed up with the Estado Novo dictatorship, he left for Brazil, where he settled in 1953 and became a Brazilian citizen in 1960.

The photographs from those years are what made him known in Portugal. They are black-and-white portraits full of masks, shadows and superimpositions, in which people's faces are never just faces. According to the newspaper Postal do Algarve, Lemos explained that, after a first automatic gesture, the rest of the photographic work was thought through and planned, almost like writing. In Brazil he was above all an abstract painter, draughtsman and graphic designer: he won the drawing prize at the São Paulo Biennial in 1957, taught at the Faculty of Architecture and Urbanism of the University of São Paulo, headed the Brazilian Industrial Design Association and, with a Gulbenkian grant, studied calligraphy in Japan in 1963. In Portugal, he received the Prémio Nacional de Fotografia (National Photography Prize) in 2001.

A curiosity: Fernando Lemos's most famous self-portrait does not show his face, but a light bulb and the tarot card «The Hanged Man». According to the Diário de Notícias, he tried to use it as his passport photograph, and the authorities refused. In Brazil, the same newspaper says, they jokingly called him «the guy Salazar sent us». He died in São Paulo in December 2019, aged 93. He used to sum up his life like this: «I have two homelands, one that made me and another that I help to make.»

03 / Did you know…?

Did you know that the first Christmas card to be sold was born in 1843, in London, because a man did not have time to answer his letters?

Did you know that Christmas cards were born out of a lack of time? In December 1843, in London, Henry Cole had a pile of letters waiting for a reply. To get his season's greetings out of the way, he asked a painter friend, John Callcott Horsley, to design a card to be printed, to which he only had to add the name. It is considered the first commercial Christmas card.

Continue readingShow less

Cole was no ordinary man. A civil servant, educator and inventor, he helped to reform the British postal service in the 1840s, with the penny stamp, which made sending letters cheap, and he was later the first director of the museum now called the Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A), in London. According to the V&A, Cole's diary records that Horsley brought him the design on 17 December 1843. In the centre, hand-coloured, is the Cole family, three generations raising a toast; around it, in black-and-white scenes, people giving food and clothing to the poor. Underneath, the words «A Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year to You». Horsley signed it with a small self-portrait and the date «Xmasse, 1843».

A thousand copies were printed. Cole used the ones he needed and put the rest on sale at a shilling each, which was expensive at the time. The result: according to the V&A, it was a commercial failure. And there was controversy. In one corner of the design, a child drinks wine from an adult's glass, and the temperance movement, which campaigned against alcohol, did not like it at all, says Mental Floss magazine.

The idea, however, caught on. With new printing techniques, cards became cheap and, according to the Historic UK website, 11.5 million were already being produced in the United Kingdom by 1880. Few copies of Cole's card survive, between a dozen and about twenty, depending on the source. In 2013, one of them, which had belonged to Cole himself, was sold at auction for £22,000, according to the V&A. In other words: the first Christmas card cost a shilling and today it is worth a small fortune.

04 / Culture on the move

WOOL: for 15 years Covilhã's street art festival has been painting the town's walls, and this year it got more than a thousand people crocheting to cover a house

Origins
2011, in Covilhã; created by the siblings Lara and Pedro Seixo Rodrigues, who grew up in the town, with Elisabet Carceller
Where
Covilhã (Castelo Branco district), mainly in the historic centre
Field
Street art and public art: murals, tile panels, installations; also exhibitions, concerts, residencies and guided tours
Participants
Portuguese and foreign artists (131 works by 79 artists in the first ten editions, according to Lusa) and the local community; Bordalo II's first mural was born at WOOL
Now
13th edition from 11 to 21 June 2026, for the festival's 15th anniversary, with the community project «A Nossa Casa» (Our House); the murals can be seen in the street

In Covilhã, the walls tell stories. For 15 years, WOOL – Covilhã Arte Urbana, presented as the oldest street art festival in the country, has been inviting Portuguese and foreign artists to paint the Serra da Estrela town. The 13th edition took place in June and brought new murals, tile panels and a project in which more than a thousand people, aged from 3 to 102, made crochet and knitted squares to cover a house in the historic centre.

Continue readingShow less

It all began in 2011. Lara Seixo Rodrigues, a curator and producer, and her brother Pedro grew up in Covilhã and studied architecture in Lisbon. Inspired by festivals such as Fame, in Italy, and Asalto, in Spain, they wanted to bring the idea to their town. According to the Diário de Notícias, the project was submitted to the Direção-Geral das Artes (the Directorate-General for the Arts) that year and came 8th among the ten chosen. The first edition had four artists, two Portuguese and two foreign, painting one at a time. The name, «lã» in Portuguese, points to the history of a wool-industry town, and the Observador describes the festival as a way of preserving the memory of an industrial territory, partly abandoned.

The idea is simple: put art in the street and bring artists closer to the people who live there. In ten editions, according to Lusa, 52 Portuguese and 27 foreign artists left 131 works in the town, and the street art trail has dozens of murals, which can be seen on guided tours on foot or by tuk-tuk. It was at WOOL that Bordalo II painted his first mural. The road has not always been easy: after the initial support from DGArtes, there were years without public funding, and the festival also relies on support from private companies. As Lara Seixo Rodrigues tells the DN, for street art to work, it «has to be communicated every day».

This year, the theme was participation. Murals were painted by Ben Johnston (South Africa), Tellas (Italy), the Canadian collective Nasarimba and the Portuguese Projeto Ruído and Mariana, a Miserável, in a mural for the centenary of the Orfeão da Covilhã choir. There were also ten tile panels and installations. The project «A Nossa Casa» (Our House) brought together more than 5,000 crochet and knitted squares, made over months by more than a thousand people in more than a hundred institutions around the country, such as care homes and senior academies. Why does this matter? In an inland town, far from the big centres, WOOL shows that contemporary art can change the landscape and get different generations working together. «We must keep investing heavily in community involvement and participation», the organiser told the newspaper UrbietOrbi.
