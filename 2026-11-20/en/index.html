Shenzhen: Xi, Trump and Putin close their summit with a «peace declaration» that says nothing about Ukraine or Iran

Fact: summit ended on 19 Nov with a joint declaration · no concrete agreements on the wars · no new meetings announced · 19/11/2026

The first three-way summit between the leaders of the US, China and Russia ended on Thursday 19 November in Shenzhen, in southern China, with a «trilateral peace declaration». Xi Jinping, Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin met for about an hour and a half. The text covers UN reform, more oil production and dialogue on nuclear risks — but does not mention the war in Ukraine or the one in Iran, according to Euronews and AP.

What does the declaration say? Besides those three points, the three countries defend the immunity of state officials and criticise the International Criminal Court and other courts which, they say, «abuse their jurisdiction», according to AP and Euronews. The text was released by Chinese state media and by Russian officials. There were no joint statements to journalists at the end: the three posed for the family photo without shaking hands, and Trump went straight to the airport.

The tone was upbeat. Trump said the three are «making history» and are uniquely placed to talk about nuclear weapons. Xi spoke of a «positive, constructive and fruitful» summit and argued that nuclear weapons should be banned and destroyed. Putin called it an «important chapter» in diplomacy and said the outcome exceeded expectations. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov admitted that Ukraine was not «the central topic».

In Europe, the reaction was one of suspicion. French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said the three leaders «disagree on everything» and so «cannot agree on anything», according to Euronews. There are fears that the great powers will decide on Ukraine without Kyiv or the Europeans. Hours after the summit ended, Russia launched a massive missile and drone attack on Ukraine (see next story).

European Commission confirms cyberattack on staff systems using an AI tool; it is not yet known whether data was stolen

Attack confirmed by the Commission · theft of personal data unconfirmed · attacker unknown · 19/11/2026

The European Commission has been hit by a cyberattack that knocked out, for several days, the systems staff use to check their pay, request leave and log their hours. The Commission confirmed it on Thursday 19 November, after the story was reported by Politico. According to spokesperson Thomas Regnier, the attackers used ARTEX, a publicly available artificial intelligence (AI) tool designed to look for weaknesses in computer systems.

What was affected? Mainly Sysper, the human resources platform, and the system for reimbursing medical expenses under the staff health insurance scheme. Several applications were switched off as a precaution and most are working again; salaries were paid as normal. According to Euronews, Regnier said the attack was detected on 22 October; Politico, quoted by Tek, reports disruption lasting more than ten days. CERT-EU, the cybersecurity team of the EU institutions, and the European Data Protection Supervisor have been informed.

What we know — and what we don't. An EU official told Politico that the Commission believes it has stopped most of the attack, but does not yet know how far the damage goes. These systems hold staff names, home addresses, contact details and pay grades. Speaking to Euronews, Regnier said there was no evidence of a data breach affecting staff «as a whole» and that there had been no ransom demand; he would not say who was behind it or whether the attack is still going on.

It is not the first case this year: in January the system that manages the Commission's mobile phones was attacked and, in March, about 92 GB of data was stolen from the cloud service hosting the Europa.eu websites. As for ARTEX, it is an open-source program that uses AI agents to test the security of systems; according to Tek, the FBI warned on 13 November that several groups have been using it in attacks since August.

Germany raises its cybersecurity alert to «tense to critical»: with AI, attacks are faster and cheaper

Official assessment in the BSI's annual report (19 Nov) · does not refer to a specific attack · 19/11/2026

Germany now considers the cyber threat to be «tense to critical», one step above last year's «tense». The assessment comes from the BSI, Germany's federal IT security agency, in its annual report presented in Berlin on Thursday 19 November with the interior minister, Alexander Dobrindt. The main reason: artificial intelligence (AI) is making attacks «ever faster, cheaper and more targeted».

Continue readingShow less

The figures help explain it. The time between a flaw being made public and the first known attack exploiting it fell from 53 days to 25 and, in the second quarter of 2026, to eight; Dobrindt said there are already cases of just a few hours. The BSI reports around 75,400 infected devices a day in Germany to internet providers, 89% more than before — 88% are Android, and some cheap uncertified devices come with malicious software already installed at the factory.

The BSI's president, Claudia Plattner, called for an end to «complacency» and said AI has already found flaws that went unnoticed for around 20 years. She named Russia, China, Iran and North Korea as the origin of many attacks, although it is not always clear whether the states direct them or merely tolerate them. Small and medium-sized businesses are the most vulnerable target, because there are so many of them and they are often less well protected.

There are positive signs too: according to Reuters, the report acknowledges progress in IT security regulation and says operators of critical infrastructure, subject to stricter rules, are generally better protected — though still with «room for improvement». The report also recalls the attacks in October and November on refrigeration systems in supermarkets in Germany and other European countries.

European Central Bank compares AI giants' borrowing to the dotcom and subprime bubbles

Warning in the ECB's financial stability review (18 Nov) · risk assessment, not a forecast of a crisis · 18/11/2026

The European Central Bank (ECB) is worried about how the race for artificial intelligence (AI) is being paid for. In the financial stability review published on Wednesday 18 November, it warns that the big US tech companies are increasingly financing data centres with debt. That shift, the bank writes, recalls the bubble in internet companies (the «dotcoms», around 2000) and the one in high-risk mortgage lending (the «subprime» bubble that led to the 2008 crisis).

Continue readingShow less

The numbers help explain why. According to the ECB, companies such as Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Oracle and Anthropic had, by mid-2026, more than $4 trillion in commitments that do not show up as debt in their accounts: long-term contracts with those who build data centres and buy chips. The bank also points to «circular financing» (chipmakers financing their own customers), which makes real demand hard to measure, and to the short life of AI chips: one to three years, while the debt that pays for them lasts much longer.

And Europe? The ECB says euro area banks are not significantly exposed, but that European investment funds and households hold more and more shares and debt in these companies and could suffer losses. If the cycle goes wrong in the US, it warns, the euro area economy would shrink too. The bank notes that, so far, tech companies' profits have kept up with expectations, and that the dotcom bubble caused only a mild recession because it was paid for mainly with shares rather than debt.

British tribunal forces the government to admit it demanded access to Apple's encrypted data

Tribunal ruling (19 Nov) · the order's legality will only be tried in 2027 · 19/11/2026

The British government can no longer refuse to talk about it. The Investigatory Powers Tribunal, the court that oversees surveillance and the intelligence services in the UK, ruled on Thursday 19 November that the Home Office cannot keep to its position of «neither confirm nor deny» that it sent Apple a secret order to provide access to customers' encrypted data.

Continue readingShow less

Why does it matter? These orders, known as «technical capability notices», can force a company to create a way for the authorities to reach protected data. Apple refuses to open such a «back door» in its encryption and, because of this dispute, stopped offering Advanced Data Protection in the UK, the option that encrypts copies stored in iCloud end to end.

According to City A.M., the tribunal found the government's national security assessment flawed, because the order's existence was already widely known — and had even been confirmed to newspapers through leaks the tribunal describes as «authorised». The technical details of the order remain secret.

What next? Apple's complaint, and a parallel one by the privacy campaign groups Privacy International and Liberty, goes to a full hearing in the first half of 2027, largely open to the public. In other words: the case gains transparency, but the main question — whether the order is lawful — is still to be decided.

UN court finds for the first time that a state committed genocide: Myanmar, against the Rohingya

Fact: ICJ judgment on 19 Nov, backed by 9 of the 15 judges · final, no appeal · compensation still to be set · 19/11/2026

The International Court of Justice (ICJ), the UN's main court, in The Hague, ruled on Thursday 19 November that Myanmar (formerly Burma) committed genocide against the Muslim Rohingya minority. It is the first time the court has declared that a state committed genocide. The decision was backed by nine of the 15 judges and cannot be appealed, according to Al Jazeera and Observador.

Continue readingShow less

The case was brought to the court by the Gambia in 2019. It concerns the 2017 military offensive in Rakhine State, which killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee to neighbouring Bangladesh. The judges found killings, sexual violence — also used to prevent births within the group — and the systematic destruction of villages to be proven. The court's president, Yuji Iwasawa, said the court was «fully convinced» that acts covered by the Genocide Convention had taken place.

What changes? Myanmar must stop the violence, investigate and try those responsible, allow the safe and voluntary return of refugees, and rebuild or return what was destroyed. Compensation will be decided later, and the country must submit a report to the court within two years. The problem is that the ICJ has no way of enforcing compliance: that would fall to the UN Security Council, where China and Russia can shield Myanmar, Observador notes.

For the Rohingya, it is a long-awaited symbolic victory. About 1.2 million live in refugee camps in Cox's Bazar, in Bangladesh. The Gambia's lead counsel spoke of a «landmark victory». Six judges dissented, arguing that the majority used a less demanding standard of proof than usual. The decision may carry weight in other genocide cases at the ICJ, such as the one South Africa is bringing against Israel.

03 / Did you know…?

Did you know that the best-selling café chair of all time had only six wooden parts and travelled flat-packed, 36 at a time, in a one-cubic-metre crate?

Did you know that you have very probably sat on a chair designed in 1859? It is called No. 14, is known as the «café chair» or «bistro chair» and was created by the German cabinetmaker Michael Thonet, in Vienna. It is made of bent beech wood, with a round cane seat and an arched back. By 1930 more than 50 million had been made. It is still manufactured today.

Continue readingShow less

The secret is in the wood. In the 1830s, Thonet began bending wood instead of carving it. In 1855 he worked out how to do it with solid wood: he softened beech rods with steam and bent them in moulds, with a metal strip fixed to the outside so the wood would not crack. It was cheaper than gluing together several thin sheets, and his patents gave him a near-monopoly on the technique for years. The main factory was in Moravia, in today's Czech Republic, close to beech forests and the railway.

Then came the idea that changed the furniture trade. The No. 14 chair has just six wooden parts — two front legs, one piece forming the back legs and backrest, an inner arch, the seat and a foot ring — held together with ten screws and two nuts. So it could travel in pieces: one crate of one cubic metre held 36 chairs, which were assembled by shops or distributors at their destination. Transport became much cheaper, and the chair reached cafés all over the world. According to the Victoria and Albert Museum, in London, it was among the first pieces of furniture to be mass-produced and shipped knocked-down — a grandmother of the shelves we now buy in flat boxes.

It set an example. According to the Design Museum, in London, the architect Le Corbusier is said to have remarked that nothing more elegant and more practical had ever been created. In 1869 the patent expired and No. 14 became one of the most copied chairs in the world. In Vienna, cafés such as the Frauenhuber and the Tirolerhof still use it. And the current version, named 214, celebrated its 160th birthday in 2019.

A week from now, from Friday 27 to Sunday 29 November, the Arquivo Municipal de Lisboa – Fotográfico, Lisbon's municipal photographic archive on Rua da Palma, will fill up with photography books. It is the 16th Lisbon Photobook Fair (Feira do Livro de Fotografia de Lisboa), also called the Feira do Fotolivro, a yearly gathering of photographers, small publishers and specialist booksellers. Entry is free. It opens on Friday from 17:00 to 20:00 and at the weekend from 11:00 to 20:00.

First, what is a photobook? It is a book in which the photographs are not illustrations: they are what tells the story, through their order, their size and the dialogue between pages. Many are made in small print runs, by the authors themselves. The fair was born to give this world a space. Behind it is an informal group of people linked to photography — in 2015 it called itself «Os Novos Suspeitos» (The New Suspects) — which that year, at the 6th edition, gained the support of Lisbon City Council's photographic archive. The fair describes itself as an experimental space to show, sell and discuss photobooks, and to build new audiences.

How does it work? There is a market of new and rare books, with publishers and antiquarian booksellers; an exhibition of «dummies», the prototypes of books that do not yet exist, which visitors can leaf through and comment on; a space for self-publishing authors; and presentations of projects, finished or in progress. This year, Time Out Lisboa mentions, among others, Ghost Editions, XYZ Books, STET, Pierre von Kleist and the Alexandria bookshop. Past editions have had talks, workshops and even a guest country: Lithuania in 2014 and Brazil in 2015.

Why does it matter? Portugal has few bookshops devoted to photography, and many photobooks never reach ordinary bookshops. For more than a decade the fair has been a yearly meeting point for those who make and those who seek out these books, and a place where young photographers show work not yet published. It also leaves its mark on the archive itself: in 2015, most of the books from the Brazilian exhibition stayed afterwards in the Arquivo's library, where they can be consulted free of charge.
